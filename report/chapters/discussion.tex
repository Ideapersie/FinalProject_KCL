\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

\section{What the Findings Mean Together}
\label{sec:disc-synthesis}

Read separately the results of Chapter~\ref{ch:eval} are a mixed report card,
since the gated policy beats always-retrieve, trails closed-book on multiple
choice, leads on open-ended, and costs more wall-clock time than either. Read
together they assemble into one argument, which is that retrieval is not a
free safety net and that a gate's value is therefore protection rather than
efficiency.

The starting observation is that injecting topically relevant but
non-decisive context makes the model worse on questions it already answers
correctly, at a ratio of 3.5 broken answers for every one fixed
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-distraction}). The mechanism is displacement rather
than noise, since the model defers to the provided context exactly as RAG
prompting instructs it to and abandons parametric knowledge that was correct,
so the standard assumption that retrieved context is at worst neutral is false
for this model class.

The natural objection is that the effect is really absence, meaning that
retrieval hurts because the corpus does not contain the answer. Three lines of
evidence rule that out as the primary cause. Retrieval fixes \fixLlama{},
\fixQwenSeven{} and \fixQwenFourteen{} closed-book errors at 3B, 7B and 14B,
which a corpus without answers could not do, and it measurably helps the
weakest model on open-ended questions. Decisively, the harm grows with
capability (Section~\ref{sec:scaling-mechanism}), whereas a corpus that merely
lacked information would damage a stronger model less. Absence cannot produce
that gradient, and displacement is its signature.

If retrieval can be poison, the value of a gate is protection rather than the
efficiency the adaptive-retrieval literature has largely framed it as, and the
sharpest evidence is the protective-skip result of
Section~\ref{sec:eval-protective}. The gate's real job on this evidence is to
locate the boundary of the model's parametric knowledge and defend everything
inside it, which also resolves the apparent contradiction between the two
question types. MIRAGE's multiple-choice questions sit largely inside the 3B
model's parametric knowledge, so retrieval can only distract, while PubMedQA's
research questions sit largely outside it, so retrieval adds information. One
boundary, two sides, and one gate whose task is to tell them apart. The
refuted ablation hypothesis (Section~\ref{sec:eval-ablation}) folds into the
same picture, since the probe-boosting variants fail by retrieving more, so
three independent analyses converge on one conclusion from different
directions.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

The following limitations are ordered roughly by how much they constrain the
conclusions.

\paragraph{Statistical power.} $n=\nQuestions{}$ per condition, one seed and
one run per configuration. The 3B accuracy gap of \diffPfivePone{}pp carries a
95\% CI of \diffPfivePoneCI{}, which crosses zero, so this study is not
powered to establish the accuracy claim at that scale and the abstract and
conclusion phrase it as consistent with an improvement. The retrieval-cost
halving is a count and is exact for the sample. The high-risk stratum ($n=6$,
Wilson intervals of roughly $\pm 30$pp) supports no between-policy comparison
at all.

\paragraph{Scaling arm: three points, one family.} Three points are a curve
through one Llama model and two Qwen models rather than a claim about model
families in general, and because the two larger points come from the same
family a family effect cannot be separated from a scale effect between them.
The curve is also visibly saturating, since the closed-book gain from 7B to
14B (\scaleGainLarge{}pp) is a fraction of the 3B-to-7B gain
(\scaleGainSmall{}pp), so extrapolating it to the higher safety bars would be
unwarranted.

\paragraph{Corpus completeness and retrieval quality.} The distraction result
does not exonerate the corpus. StatPearls was unavailable at build time, so
the index is a subset of the full MedRAG corpus, and the answer-bearing
passage is retrieved for only a minority of questions even when the gated
answer is correct. There is therefore genuine retrieval-quality headroom, and
the claim defended here is the narrower one, that on this corpus and retriever
the dominant failure mode is displacement rather than absence. Whether a
better retrieval stack would flip any multiple-choice ordering is untested.

\paragraph{Retrieval budgets are not matched across models.} Llama's gated
policy retrieved on \retPfiveMcq{} of queries against Qwen's
\retQwenPfiveMcq{}, a gap of \budgetGapMcq{}pp, for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:scaling-budget}. The consequence is worth stating plainly:
the claim that selective retrieval beats always-retrieve at a matched budget
is established within each model and not across them, because the cross-model
comparison confounds scale with retrieval rate. Only the closed-book policies,
which retrieve 0\% by definition, are cleanly comparable between models.

\paragraph{Gate thresholds are task-specific, and were not treated as such.}
Thresholds were fitted on multiple-choice drafts and reused unchanged on
open-ended drafts, which is unsound because the two draft types have signal
distributions that do not coincide. On Qwen the reuse was catastrophic and
gated P5 degenerated into always-retrieve P1, so no open-ended
selective-retrieval result can be drawn from that run. Llama avoided total
saturation only because its $\tau = 0.70$ happens to fall inside its
open-ended distribution, and even then it overshot its intended budget. The
general lesson is that a gate threshold is a property of a model and a task
format, and the method as implemented encodes only the former.

\paragraph{The open-ended metric barely discriminates.} Every open-ended
condition scores between $0.19$ and $0.26$ mean token-$F_1$ while the
underlying behaviour ranges from answering essentially every question to
declining \retPoneOpen{} of them, and refusals average $0.217$ against
$0.244$ for substantive answers. Token-$F_1$ against a paragraph-length gold
answer rewards fluent medical prose whether or not it addresses the question,
so it supports relative comparison between policies and never absolute quality
claims. The problem compounds with a corpus-benchmark mismatch, since PubMedQA
asks about the findings of specific studies the textbook-and-abstract corpus
largely does not contain, so the open-ended arm partly measures corpus
coverage rather than gating.

\paragraph{Remaining constraints.} Four smaller limitations are recorded
together. The tier-adaptive degradation mechanism is implemented and unit
tested but only the medium hardware tier was physically run, so the promised
three-device benchmarking was not delivered. Multiple-choice evaluation uses
one MIRAGE subset and open-ended evaluation one dataset, which limits
generality. Energy figures are \texttt{codecarbon} software estimates rather
than metered hardware measurements. And the clinical-risk tagger is a keyword
rule set with recall gaps on implicitly high-risk questions, which is partly
why the high-risk stratum is only $n=6$, while the 70/80/90\% bars themselves
are stipulated rather than derived from clinical evidence.

\subsection{Off-by-one in the entropy gate's logit slice}
\label{sec:limitation-entropy-offset}

The entropy gate slices generated-token rows from
\texttt{llama-cpp-python}'s \texttt{\_scores} buffer starting at
\texttt{len(tokenize(prompt))}, which is one row too high because
\texttt{tokenize()} prepends a beginning-of-sequence token the completion's
context does not contain. The discrepancy was confirmed directly, since under
greedy decoding the $\arg\max$ of each logit row must be the token that row
produced, and for a 48-token draft the slice beginning at
$n_{\text{prompt}} - 1$ reproduced all 48 generated tokens while the slice
beginning at $n_{\text{prompt}}$ reproduced none. The practical consequence is
that $\bar{H}$ averages generated tokens $2 \ldots N$ rather than
$1 \ldots N$, and on a representative clinical question the omitted first
token was the draft's highest-entropy one at $H = 3.28$ nats, which lowered
the gate signal from $0.763$ to $0.709$ against $\tau = 0.70$.

This does not invalidate the reported results, because the bias is
deterministic and identical across every question and every model, $\tau$ was
calibrated on the same shifted quantity so the retrieval budget it produces is
the budget actually delivered, and the budget-matching procedure inherits the
identical bias at all three scales. What narrows is the definitional claim,
since the quantity this work calls mean draft entropy is the mean over the
draft's second and subsequent tokens. Correcting it was judged the greater
risk at the point of discovery, because re-slicing would move an unknown
number of near-threshold decisions across $\tau$ and require full
recalibration and re-evaluation on all three models.

\section{Implications for Offline Clinical Deployment}
\label{sec:disc-deployment}

\paragraph{Do not deploy at this scale.} A quantised 3B model, gated or not,
is \gapToLowBar{}pp short of even the lowest stipulated bar, and scaling
improves this without resolving it. This deployment class is not fit for
clinical decision support beyond the lowest-risk stratum, and the honest
output of the evaluation is the measured distance rather than a product. The
professional and regulatory consequences are taken up in
Chapter~\ref{ch:lsep}.

\paragraph{Where the method is useful today.} Gating earns its keep wherever
retrieval is the expensive or risky resource, which covers cost-aware routing
in on-premises deployments under data-sovereignty constraints, edge devices
where a metered or slow retrieval backend dominates cost, and any pipeline
whose operator wants an auditable record of why the system did or did not
consult external evidence. The gate's decision payload is logged on every
query, which makes the retrieval decision reviewable after the fact in a way
that end-to-end trained routers are not.

\paragraph{Interpretability as the deployable asset.} The per-token entropy
attribution of Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution}, surfaced through the
interface of Section~\ref{sec:impl-demo}, points at something that may matter
more than a small accuracy delta in a regulated setting, which is showing
where the model was uncertain. The caveat travels with it, since on this model
raw entropy highlights phrasing rather than clinical content, so this is a
promising direction with a demonstrated limitation rather than a finished
feature. In a domain where automation bias is a documented harm
(Chapter~\ref{ch:lsep}), an uncertainty display a clinician can audit is
plausibly worth more than two points of accuracy.
