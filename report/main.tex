\begin{abstract}
% DRAFT abstract (~250 w), 2026-07-18 — leads with the finding, per feedback.
Retrieval-augmented generation is the standard remedy for hallucination in
medical question answering, but this dissertation finds that retrieval is not
a free safety net: on multiple-choice questions a quantised 3B model already
answers correctly, retrieved context \emph{broke} 46 answers and fixed only
13. Selective retrieval is therefore studied not as a cost optimisation but
as protection. A training-free three-gate ensemble --- mean token entropy,
top-two logit margin, and a two-draft hallucination probe --- decides per
query whether to retrieve, requiring no fine-tuning, no labelled data, and no
second model, targeting fully offline, CPU-only clinical deployments. On 200
MIRAGE multiple-choice questions over a 425,847-chunk medical corpus, the
gated policy reaches \accPfiveMcq{} against \accPoneMcq{} for
always-retrieve at half the retrieval calls (\retPfiveMcq{}); the cost
halving is exact, while the accuracy gain (\diffPfivePone{}pp, 95\% CI
\diffPfivePoneCI{}) is suggestive rather than established. On the questions
retrieval poisons, the gate's skip decision scored 19/19. Three further
findings shape the conclusions: literature-default gate thresholds are
physically unreachable on a quantised model and must be recalibrated per
model; on open-ended PubMedQA the ordering inverts and retrieval helps
(gated $F_1$ \fOnePfiveOpen{} versus closed-book \fOnePthreeOpen{}); and no
policy clears even the \barLow{} low-risk clinical bar --- closed-book tops
out at \accPthreeMcq{}, quantifying an \gapToLowBar{}pp distance between
this deployment class and clinical acceptability. All reported numbers are
generated from raw run logs under a drift-guarded, 180+-test reproducibility
harness.
\end{abstract}
